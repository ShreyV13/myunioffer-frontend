Economics

Almost every Economics applicant says the same thing. "I'm fascinated by how the economy works." Admissions tutors have read that line thousands of times. Here's what actually separates strong applicants.

What tutors actually look for

The real criteria.
Not the prospectus version.

Based on what students who got offers actually did differently.

Tutors don't expect you to understand game theory or monetary policy at degree level. They expect you to look at the world and ask economic questions about it. Why do some products cost more at airports? Why does raising minimum wage sometimes not reduce employment the way textbooks predict? Why do people make financial decisions that seem irrational? If you can take something you've observed in real life and frame it as an economic question, you're already thinking like an economist.

Mention specific mathematical skills or concepts from your A-levels that excite you, not just that you "enjoy problem-solving." Genuine curiosity beyond the syllabus.

Mathematical reasoning matters more than most applicants realise. Economics at university level is heavily quantitative. Top programmes (LSE, Cambridge, Warwick, UCL) expect strong maths alongside economics. If you're applying to these universities, your maths ability is as important as your interest in the subject. Mention specific mathematical skills or concepts from your A-levels that excite you, not just that you "enjoy problem-solving."

Genuine curiosity beyond the syllabus. Every applicant studies the same A-level content. What tutors want to see is that you've gone further on your own. Read a book that challenged a concept from your course. Found an article that presented an argument you disagreed with. The key is engagement with the material, not just consumption of it.

From 2026 entry, UCAS replaced the freeform essay with three structured questions. 4,000 characters total, minimum 350 per question. For Economics, spend the majority of your characters on questions 1 and 2. That's where your academic engagement lives.

Question 1 (why economics): Start with something specific. Not "I've always been interested in how markets work." Maybe a news story about inflation that made you question the trade-offs policymakers face. Maybe growing up in a family business and noticing pricing decisions that didn't match what your textbook said. UCAS's own guide suggests picking "something slightly more obscure" rather than the books every applicant references. Whatever you reference, have an opinion about it.

Question 2 (how your studies prepared you): This is where your maths background matters. Talk about specific areas that connect to economics: statistical analysis, probability, calculus. If you've done an EPQ related to economics, discuss it here. Draw connections from other subjects too. History teaches you to evaluate competing explanations. Psychology introduces behavioural concepts that challenge classical assumptions.

Question 3 (outside education): One strong example beats three weak ones. If you've entered the Royal Economic Society essay competition, talk about your argument. If you've done a MOOC, talk about one concept that challenged your thinking. If you have a part-time job, connect something you observed to economic theory.

Work experience

What actually counts.

It's not about how many hours. It's about what you noticed.

BSc vs BA Economics is a distinction most applicants overlook. Some universities offer BSc Economics (more quantitative, heavier on maths and statistics) and others offer BA Economics (broader, more room for qualitative analysis and policy). Some offer both.

If you're studying Further Maths and enjoy the quantitative side, BSc programmes at LSE, UCL, or Warwick will suit you. If you're more interested in economic policy, history of economic thought, or development economics, BA programmes or joint honours courses might be a better fit.

This matters for your personal statement because the skills and interests you emphasise should match the type of programme you're applying to. A BSc-focused statement should demonstrate mathematical aptitude. A BA-focused statement can lean more into policy debates and theoretical arguments.

Recommended reading

What to read before you apply.

The standard recommendations are Freakonomics, The Undercover Economist, and Thinking, Fast and Slow. These are fine books but they're on every applicant's list. If you mention one, you need to go deeper than a summary.

Beyond books: The Economist, the FT, and podcasts like More or Less (BBC) or Planet Money (NPR) keep you engaged with current economic debates. Being able to reference a recent policy decision or economic event shows you're paying attention to economics as a living subject.

CORE Econ's "The Economy" is designed specifically as an introduction to university-level economics and is used by several UK universities in their first year. Completing even part of it gives you genuine talking points about economic models and their limitations.

01
Why Nations FailAcemoglu & Robinson

The argument that institutions, not geography or culture, determine economic outcomes gives you a rich thread to pull on. Connect it to specific countries, challenge it with counterexamples, link it to current policy debates.

02
MisbehavingRichard Thaler

Goes deeper than Nudge into how behavioural economics challenged classical assumptions. The tension between rational actor models and real human behaviour connects to almost every area of economics.

03
The Economics of InequalityThomas Piketty

The shorter one, not Capital. Accessible, specific, and gives you concrete arguments about redistribution, taxation, and what "fair" means in economic terms.

04
Everything Is PredictableTom Chivers

UCAS specifically recommends this. Covers Bayesian thinking and probability. Unusual enough that mentioning it signals you've gone beyond the standard reading list.

05
The Undercover EconomistTim Harford

Solid introduction to microeconomic thinking. Better than Freakonomics for personal statements because the arguments are more directly connected to core economic concepts.

The Royal Economic Society runs an annual essay competition for students. It's one of the strongest things you can put on an Economics application because it directly demonstrates constructing an economic argument in writing. Check their website for the current year's topic and deadline.

The Bank of England's educational resources and competitions are worth looking into. Some schools participate in the Target Two Point Zero competition, which simulates monetary policy decisions.

If you can't find a formal competition, write something independently. A blog post analysing a current economic issue. A short essay responding to an argument from a book you've read. The act of writing about economics, and being able to reference it in your statement, is what matters.

MOOCs can be valuable if you pick the right one. CORE Econ's "The Economy" is the strongest choice. Completing even part of it and discussing a specific model or concept it introduced you to is more impressive than listing three courses you barely remember.

Exam preparation

The admissions test.

The TMUA (Test of Mathematics for University Admission) is used by several top universities for Economics, including Cambridge, LSE, Imperial, Warwick, Durham, and UCL. If any of your UCAS choices are on this list, you need to prepare.

The TMUA tests mathematical reasoning and problem-solving, not memorised formulas. It assesses whether you can think mathematically in unfamiliar situations. You can sit it in October or January, but only your first score counts. Cambridge and Oxford applicants must take the October sitting.

Registration opens 20 July 2026. The October sitting runs 12-16 October. The January sitting runs 4-8 January. Start preparing 4-6 weeks before. Practice papers are on the UAT-UK website.

Important: not all Economics programmes require the TMUA. Many excellent universities don't use it at all. If your maths is strong, target TMUA universities to make it an advantage. If maths isn't your strongest subject, you still have plenty of strong programmes to choose from.

Choosing your universities

Strategy matters as much as strength.

Economics is offered at nearly every UK university but the programmes vary enormously. Research whether maths is integrated into the course or taught separately. Whether the programme requires TMUA, and how heavily it's weighted. Whether the programme leans quantitative (LSE, Cambridge, Warwick, UCL) or allows more breadth.

Whether they interview. Cambridge and some Oxford courses (PPE, Economics and Management) interview. Most other universities make offers based on your application alone.

If you're applying to quantitative programmes and your statement doesn't mention mathematical skills or enthusiasm for quantitative analysis, that's a red flag. Tutors will wonder if you know what you're signing up for.

Common mistakes

What kills most applications.

01

Writing a personal statement that could apply to any social science. If you could swap "economics" for "politics" and it still works, it's not specific enough. Every claim should connect to an economic concept, model, or question.

02

Neglecting the maths angle. If you're applying to quantitative programmes without mentioning mathematical skills, tutors will wonder if you know what you're signing up for.

03

Referencing Freakonomics without going deeper. It's the most commonly cited book on Economics statements. A vague "it showed me economics is everywhere" line actively hurts you because it signals surface-level reading.

04

Confusing interest in business with interest in economics. Economics is an academic discipline. If your statement reads like you want to study business management, you're applying for the wrong course.

05

Not preparing for the TMUA when applying to universities that use it. Students who don't practise often score significantly below their potential.
