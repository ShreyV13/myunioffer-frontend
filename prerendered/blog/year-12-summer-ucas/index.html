What to do in Year 12 summer for your UCAS application

SV
Shrey Verma·LSE·May 2026·6 min read

Most Year 12 students will sit down in September to write their personal statement and realise they have nothing to say. They spent the summer doing bits and pieces, reading a few books off a recommended list, maybe entering a competition or two, and none of it connects. The statement ends up reading like a CV with adjectives.

It doesn't have to go that way. But the fix starts now, not in September.

Depth beats breadth. Every time.

Here's something nobody told me in Year 12: admissions tutors don't want to see how much you've done. They want to see how you think about what you've done.

When preparing for my own application, I read too many books. Nudge, Prisoners of Geography, Why Nations Fail. I thought a longer reading list would look better. It didn't. Why Nations Fail ended up carrying most of my personal statement. Nudge was about behavioural psychology, which had nothing to do with the argument I was making. Prisoners of Geography was interesting but didn't connect either. Two out of three books were a waste of time because I picked them to look well-read, not because they fit a thread.

Pick one book that's relevant to your course. Read it properly. Find one argument in it that you find interesting, or that you disagree with. Then follow that thread. Read an article on the same topic. Find a counterargument. Connect it to something you've noticed in real life. That's what happened with Why Nations Fail for me. One argument about institutions and economic growth led to an academic paper, which led to connecting it to things I'd actually observed growing up. That single thread gave me more usable material than Nudge and Prisoners of Geography combined.

Your experiences matter more than you think

The part of my personal statement that felt strongest wasn't about any book. It was where I connected what I'd read to places I'd actually lived and things I'd seen with my own eyes. Growing up in different regions, I'd noticed real differences in how local economies worked, how governments spent money, how opportunities were distributed. When I linked those observations to academic ideas about institutions and economic development, the statement stopped sounding like a summary of other people's work and started sounding like mine.

You have experiences like this too. Maybe you haven't thought of them as relevant yet. A part-time job where you noticed something about pricing or customer behaviour. A family situation that got you thinking about policy. A conversation that stuck with you. The stuff admissions tutors remember isn't the impressive-sounding extracurricular. It's the specific moment where a student showed they can connect ideas to the world around them.

Start writing in August, not before

Trying to write a personal statement in June or July is a mistake. You won't have enough material yet and you'll end up with a weak draft that you spend months trying to fix.

What works better: keep a notes app. Every time you read something that makes you think, write a sentence or two about your reaction. Not a summary of what you read, but what you actually thought about it. Did you agree? Why? Did something surprise you? By the time August comes around, you'll have weeks of fragments to pull from, and the statement will come together quickly because you already know what you want to say.

The students who struggle most in September are the ones who skipped this step. They have experiences and reading but no record of their own thinking, so the statement ends up being descriptive rather than reflective. Admissions tutors can tell the difference immediately.

Don't enter competitions for the sake of it

I entered essay competitions that had nothing to do with the course I was applying for. Complete waste of time. One of them was on a topic so far from my personal statement that I couldn't mention it even if I'd wanted to.

The competitions worth entering are the ones where the topic connects to your thread. If you're applying for Philosophy, a philosophy essay competition makes sense. If you're applying for Law and you enter a poetry competition, that's not going to help you. Sounds obvious, but when you're in Year 12 and panicking about having enough on your personal statement, you start saying yes to everything. Don't. Be selective.

Same goes for MOOCs and online courses. One that's directly relevant to your subject and that you actually finish and think about is worth five that you start and abandon.

It's not the whole summer

None of this needs to take over your life. During my own summer, most of the time was spent doing normal things. Sport, seeing friends, being a teenager. The application stuff happened in pockets, maybe a few hours a week of reading and noting things down, with a more concentrated push in August when writing actually started.

The students who burn themselves out trying to do everything by July are usually the ones who end up with unfocused applications. Relax. Read one good book. Follow one interesting idea. Write down what you think. That's genuinely enough if the thinking is real.
